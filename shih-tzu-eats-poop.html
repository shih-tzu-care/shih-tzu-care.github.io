Why Your Shih Tzu Eats Poop and How to Stop It: Supervision, Leave-It Training and What Owners Say Actually Worked

This guide looks at why some Shih Tzus eat their own or other dogs' stools, what a large owner survey found about the habit, and which supervision and training routines Shih Tzu owners report as useful.

If your Shih Tzu eats poop, you are in very large company, and the habit says nothing bad about your dog or about how you raised it. It is one of the most common complaints in Shih Tzu owner groups, it shows up in easily house-trained dogs, and it often survives every trick the internet suggests. What does change the picture is plain and a little boring: getting to the pile first, and teaching your dog that walking away from it pays better than eating it.

Quick answer

Most Shih Tzus that eat poop do it within seconds of going, so the fix is mostly logistics. Go out with your dog every time, call it to you for a treat the moment it finishes, pick up straight away, and practice "leave it" for the piles you cannot control. Expect to manage the habit rather than cure it: in a survey of long-term stool eaters, no training method or product stopped it in more than 4% of dogs.

Why does my Shih Tzu eat poop?

The honest answer is that nobody has pinned down a single cause, and the best data we have rules out most of the popular explanations. Stool eating looks less like a training failure and more like a built-in tendency that some dogs have and others do not.

The largest study on the question is a pair of web surveys run by veterinary behavior researchers at UC Davis and published in 2018 as "The paradox of canine conspecific coprophagy". The first survey collected 1,552 usable responses about dogs in general. Of the 1,441 owners who answered the stool-eating questions, 16% had seen their dog eat dog poop at least six times, and about 23% had seen it at least once. Roughly one dog in six is a regular.

What the survey ruled out

The researchers compared regular stool eaters with dogs that had never been seen doing it. They found no difference by age, by sex or neuter status, or by diet. House training made no difference either: 78% of the frequent stool eaters had been easy to house-train, against 82% of the dogs that never touched it. A Shih Tzu that learned the potty training routine without trouble can still be a devoted poop eater.

What did go with it

  • Greedy eating. This was the strongest link. 51.1% of the stool eaters were described by their owners as greedy eaters, compared with 28.2% of the others.
  • More dogs in the home. Stool eaters were more often found in multi-dog households, where there is simply more poop around.
  • Freshness. In the second survey, which covered 1,475 dogs seen eating stools more than ten times and at least weekly, 85% ate only stools that were no more than two days old.

That last point is the useful one. If the target is almost always fresh, then the window you have to win is short and predictable: the minute after your dog finishes.

The "wolf den" idea

The authors suggest the habit may be a leftover from wolf ancestors that cleaned droppings out of the resting area while they were still fresh. It is a hypothesis, not a proven cause, but it fits the pattern owners describe: a dog that spins round and goes for the pile before you have opened the bag.

Is poop eating a Shih Tzu thing?

Owners certainly think so, but the survey data cannot confirm it. Shih Tzus were not reported as a separate breed in the UC Davis study, so there is no published figure showing they do it more than other dogs.

The study could only compare breeds with enough dogs in the sample. By breed group, terriers and hounds were the most likely to eat stools. Among single breeds, Shetland Sheepdogs stood out, with 41% of the 27 Shelties counted as stool eaters against 17% of other breeds, while none of the 29 Poodles was. The paper gives no number for Shih Tzus.

What we do have is the breed community. A thread titled "I can't get her to stop eating poop" on the r/Shihtzu forum drew 184 comments in August 2026, and similar threads appear every few months. Owners there call it a known quirk of the breed. Treat that as shared experience, not as a statistic. Two things about Shih Tzu life probably make the habit more visible: many are raised on indoor pee pads, where the pile sits in reach until someone notices, and a long face coat makes the result hard to miss.

How to read these numbers

This is a tally of what people chose to write, not a trial. Owners with a success story are more likely to post it, and when a dog stops at fourteen months it is impossible to say whether the pumpkin or the birthday did the work. The nine "grew out of it" comments and the twelve "still doing it" comments are the best reminder of that.

The second UC Davis survey asked the owners of committed stool eaters what they had tried and whether it ended the habit. The answer was close to "nothing did."

Two things are worth noticing. First, the reward-based "leave it" had the best result on the list, and the punishing options, pepper and collars, did no better than doing nothing while being unpleasant for the dog. That is why we do not suggest hot sauce or pepper on the pile, whatever a few forum comments say. Second, "success" in this survey meant the habit went away completely. A cue that gets your dog to walk off nine piles out of ten would still count as a failure here, and yet it would change your daily life.

Trainer Victoria Stilwell reaches the same conclusion from the practical side. Her guide to poop eating calls it a tough behavior issue "with one clear solution: Clean it up!" and points out that even dogs fed high-quality diets will eat stools. So if pineapple or pumpkin did nothing for your Shih Tzu, you did not do it wrong.

Step-by-step: the supervision routine that owners rely on

The aim is simple: your dog should never get a free go at a fresh pile. Each pile it eats is a reward for the habit, and each pile you remove first is one less rehearsal.

  1. Go out every time. For the next few weeks, no solo potty trips. Take the bag with you, not "in a minute."
  2. Use a light leash, even in the yard. Six feet of leash stops the spin-and-grab that owners describe. A comfortable, well-fitted harness keeps the pressure off a small neck if your dog lunges back toward the pile.
  3. Learn the tell. Most Shih Tzus circle, sniff and hunch in the same way each time. Once you know the signs, you can be standing next to your dog before it finishes.
  4. Throw a party the second it is done. Say "yes!" in a happy voice, take two steps back and feed a treat from your hand, away from the pile. Six owners in our tally used some version of this, and all six said it helped.
  5. Pick up before you do anything else. Bag it while your dog is still chewing the treat.
  6. Keep the ground clear. Since most stool eaters go for fresh piles, a daily sweep of the yard removes nearly all of the old temptations too.
  7. Tidy the pad area. If your dog uses pee pads, change the pad as soon as it is used and try to time pad breaks for when you are in the room.

If you have more than one dog

Walk or let out the poop eater separately for a while, or keep it on leash while the other dog goes. Several owners described a dog that follows its housemate around and waits. That dog needs distance, not a lecture.

On walks

Other dogs' piles, goose droppings and rabbit pellets are harder, because you cannot remove them first. Keep the leash short through known hot spots and reward your dog for looking at you as you pass. If walks are already a struggle, our guide for a Shih Tzu that hates walks has route and pace ideas that also make these passes easier.

Step-by-step: leave-it training for a Shih Tzu that eats poop

"Leave it" does not replace supervision. It covers the moments supervision misses: a pile on the pavement, a present from the neighbor's cat. Train it with food first, well away from any poop, in sessions of two or three minutes.

  1. Closed hand. Hold a plain treat in your fist at nose level. Let your dog sniff and lick. The moment it pulls its nose back, say "yes" and give a better treat from your other hand.
  2. Open hand. Show the treat on an open palm. Close your hand if the dog dives in. Reward any pause or look away.
  3. Add the words. When your dog is backing off reliably, say "leave it" just before you present the treat.
  4. On the floor. Put the treat on the ground with your foot ready to cover it. Reward from your hand, never from the floor, so that the thing on the ground is never the prize.
  5. On the move. With your dog on leash, walk past a treat on the floor. Reward as soon as it looks at you instead.
  6. Take it outside. Repeat step five in the yard, then on a quiet walk, with boring items first.
  7. The real thing. On leash, at a distance where your dog notices a pile but can still listen, say "leave it," back away and pay generously. Close the distance over many sessions.

Shih Tzus have a reputation for being stubborn, but most are simply careful about what is worth their effort. Tiny, soft, smelly treats and very short sessions do more than repetition. If your dog is one of the greedy eaters the study describes, that appetite is on your side here. If your dog already resists handling, the same reward-first approach from our grooming biting training plan applies: small steps, and the dog always gets to succeed.

What are the most common mistakes?

Most mistakes come from treating the habit as naughtiness. Your dog is not being spiteful, and every one of these is easy to change.

  • Shouting or chasing. Chasing dogs away from stools was the most common tactic in the survey and worked for 1 to 2% of owners. It can also turn the pile into a race your dog wins by swallowing faster.
  • Scolding house accidents. One owner in our tally linked scolding to a dog that began clearing up after itself. Clean up calmly and reward outdoor successes instead.
  • Counting on a food add-in alone. Pineapple and pumpkin were a coin flip in owner reports, and marketed products scored 0 to 2% in the survey. Try one if you like, but keep supervising.
  • Putting pepper or hot sauce on the pile. Low success in the survey and unkind to a small dog's nose and mouth.
  • Stopping too soon. Owners often relax after a clean fortnight and the habit comes back. Plan on months, not days.
  • Leaving used pads down. A pad with a pile on it in an empty room is an open invitation.

How long does it take, and will my dog grow out of it?

Some do. Nine owners in our tally said their dog simply stopped, most often somewhere between six months and three years old. Twelve others were still managing dogs aged from nearly two to sixteen. The UC Davis survey found no link between age and stool eating, so do not wait for a birthday to solve it.

A fairer target is your interception rate: the share of piles you reach before your dog does. Count for a week. If you get to 9 out of 10, your dog is practicing the habit so rarely that it stops being part of daily life, even if the urge never fully goes away. The planner at the top of this page works that number out for you.

Cleaning up after a slip

Slips happen. Stay calm, skip the telling-off and deal with the face. Wipe the muzzle and beard with a damp cloth, then comb the moustache through. Our guide on how often to groom a Shih Tzu's face covers the quick daily wipe that keeps the beard clean, which makes this job much faster. A shorter face trim is worth considering while you are still working on the habit.

If an adult dog that never ate poop suddenly starts, mention it at your next vet visit. Otherwise this is a management job, and you now have a plan for it.

Daily poop patrol checklist

  • Bags and treats by the door before every trip
  • Out together, on leash if needed
  • "Yes!" and a treat away from the pile the second your dog finishes
  • Pile bagged before the treat is swallowed
  • One two-minute "leave it" session with food
  • Yard or pad area checked once more in the evening
  • Tally: piles seen today, piles your dog reached first

Frequently asked questions

Why does my Shih Tzu eat poop?

No single cause has been proven. In a UC Davis survey, stool eating was not linked to age, sex, diet or poor house training. It was linked to greedy eating and to living with other dogs, and most dogs went only for fresh stools, which is why the researchers suspect an inherited tendency.

Do Shih Tzus eat poop more than other breeds?

There is no published figure for Shih Tzus. The UC Davis study found terriers and hounds most likely to do it and did not report Shih Tzus separately. Owners in breed forums describe it as very common, but that is shared experience, not a statistic.

How do I stop my Shih Tzu from eating poop?

Go out with your dog every time, reward it for coming to you as soon as it finishes, pick up immediately and teach "leave it" for piles you cannot remove. In 80 owner comments we reviewed, immediate pick-up and supervision was the most reported method by a wide margin.

Does pineapple or pumpkin stop a dog from eating poop?

Results are mixed. In the owner comments we reviewed, three said pineapple helped and three said it did not, while four said pumpkin helped and one said it did not. In the UC Davis survey, products sold for stool eating stopped it in 0 to 2% of dogs.

Does "leave it" work for poop eating?

It helps you interrupt your dog, but it rarely ends the habit by itself. In the UC Davis survey, rewarding a "leave it alone" command had the best result of any method, yet only 4% of owners said it stopped the stool eating completely. Use it together with supervision.

Will my Shih Tzu puppy grow out of eating poop?

Some do. Nine owners in our tally said their dog stopped between about six months and three years of age, while twelve were still managing adult dogs. Since each eaten pile rewards the habit, it is better to supervise now than to wait.

Should I punish my Shih Tzu for eating poop?

No. Chasing dogs away from stools worked for only 1 to 2% of owners in the survey, and scolding can teach a dog to eat faster or to hide the evidence. Calmly remove the pile and reward your dog for moving away from it.

Shih Tzu eats poop: a short summary

When a Shih Tzu eats poop, the cause is rarely diet, spite or bad house training. About one dog in six does it regularly, nearly always with fresh piles, and no product or command has a strong record of ending it. What Shih Tzu owners report working is presence: go out together, celebrate the moment your dog finishes, pick up at once and build a "leave it" for everything else. Count your interception rate for a week, aim for nine piles out of ten, and be patient with yourself and your dog. Some dogs drop the habit as they mature. The rest can be managed so well that it hardly matters.
