Getting a Second Dog for Your Shih Tzu: Timing, Second Tzu vs Another Breed, and How Owners Handled the First Weeks

Consider getting a second dog for your Shih Tzu when your first dog can relax around a compatible dog and your household can run separate routines. A second Tzu is one candidate. A calm adult of another breed may fit better than a puppy that keeps chasing your dog after they walk away.

Quick answer

Choose for play style and the ability to settle. Arrange a neutral walk, prepare separate feeding and resting areas, and build up shared time while both dogs can disengage comfortably.

When is your Shih Tzu ready for a second dog?

Look at a normal weekday. Can you take each dog out separately, keep meals apart and give your Shih Tzu a quiet place to sleep? If you are still working through the Shih Tzu puppy potty training routine, write down who takes each dog out and where the other waits. Try running that plan against a busy day before you commit.

Watch what your dog does after the first sniff. A Shih Tzu that says hello briefly and returns to sniffing the grass has shown you something useful. A dog that keeps ducking behind your legs needs more room. Stay at the distance where both dogs can return their attention to their handlers.

Second Shih Tzu or another breed?

Start with the kind of company your dog chooses. Does your Tzu invite a gentle chase and then take a break? Do they prefer to sniff alongside another dog? Look for a candidate that can share that pace and stop when your resident dog opts out.

If a shelter or foster carer knows the candidate in a home, ask what happens around beds, bowls and doorways. Then ask to see the dog in a quiet setting. Interest in a similar-sized dog is a reason to meet, and the meeting gives you a chance to see how both dogs handle a pause. For an idea of how much grooming time to plan for a second long coat, look at the Shih Tzu face-care routine.

A puppy may suit a household that can do separate outings and supervise play while the older dog rests. If you want a quieter housemate, ask about an adult whose foster carer has seen them settle around small dogs. With a larger candidate, pay particular attention to how much body contact they use and whether they soften or back off when the smaller dog stops.

How should you introduce the dogs before coming home?

Start somewhere unfamiliar to both dogs, with a separate handler for each dog. The PetMD introduction guide describes walking in the same direction at a distance where both dogs can turn their attention back to their walkers. Close the gap gradually when both look comfortable and return to a wider gap if either becomes more tense.

  1. Pick a route with room to move. Choose a quiet stretch where you can walk in the same direction on separate lines and turn away easily. Save tight sidewalks, the front door and busy entrances for another day.
  2. Keep walking while they notice each other. Watch for loose bodies, ordinary sniffing and the ability to look away. If your Shih Tzu needs a quieter route to get comfortable outdoors, start with the route ideas in Shih Tzu Hates Walks.
  3. Keep greetings brief if both approach willingly. Walk apart afterward and see whether both dogs can return to what they were doing. If one keeps staring or lunges, end the meeting and get help planning the next one.

A walk can be the whole first meeting. You can learn whether both dogs can move comfortably in the same direction and whether either needs more room before a greeting. If you have time to arrange another walk, use it to see whether the same comfortable behavior repeats.

What should you do at home in the first weeks?

The Wisconsin Humane Society’s introduction protocol recommends feeding behind a physical barrier, separating dogs whenever you cannot supervise, starting with short interactions and keeping valuable items out of shared space during the early transition.

Arrival day. Let the newcomer explore their own space. Keep your first dog’s bed, usual walk and evening routine familiar. If the dogs stare or bark through a gate, use a closed door so both get a genuine break.

Early sessions. Start with low-key time together while you can watch both dogs. Finish after a calm stretch. Use the same room and layout a few times so you can see what changes. Keep chews and favorite toys for separate rooms.

Over the following weeks. Slowly extend shared time if the dogs repeatedly relax and leave each other alone. If you see tension around one spot, write down what happened just before it. For example, if a dog stiffens each time the other approaches the couch, arrange separate couch time and ask for help if tension recurs.

Give the resident dog a regular activity with you, even when the puppy’s schedule gets busy. The quiet activities in the Shih Tzu bonding guide are options for dogs that like company on their own terms.

Questions about getting a second dog for a Shih Tzu

What age should my Shih Tzu be before I get another dog?

Start with daily routines and behavior around the candidate. If your dog needs your full attention for walks, toilet trips or settling at home, it may be easier to establish those routines first. A dog of any age still needs a quiet retreat, and your schedule needs to cover both dogs separately.

Can a Shih Tzu live with a bigger dog?

Consider how the bigger dog behaves around a small dog. Ask for firsthand observations and arrange a controlled introduction. Look at whether the bigger dog can turn away and walk on when the smaller dog disengages. If they repeatedly rush, chase or crowd your Tzu, pause and get help evaluating the pairing.

Can the dogs share a bed on the first night?

Set up separate beds in secure areas for the first night, especially when you will be asleep. Give both dogs room to settle and keep their usual bedtime cues where possible. If either dog is fixated on the other through a gate, try a closed door for a quieter setup.

What if my first dog ignores the newcomer?

Give them room to choose how much contact they want. A dog that can nap, sniff or check in with you while the newcomer is around may already be doing well. Keep watching whether both dogs can rest, move through the room and walk away freely.

What if the puppy keeps bothering my older Shih Tzu?

End the shared session and give your older dog a private place to rest. Offer the puppy some solo play or training in a separate space. Keep the next shared session shorter and watch whether the puppy can pause or turn away when the older dog loses interest.
