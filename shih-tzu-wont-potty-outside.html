Shih Tzu Suddenly Won't Potty Outside in Cold or Wet Weather: A Retraining Plan

When a Shih Tzu won't potty outside on a cold, wet morning, the puddle by the door an hour later can feel like a protest. Treat it as information: something about the trip changed, and a small dog with feet and belly close to the ground gets a very different version of that trip than you do from under a hood.

Quick answer

Make the trip easier before you ask for more: use the same spot, give it cover, go out with your dog, say one cue and reward on the spot the moment they finish. If nothing happens, come in, supervise, and try again soon. Add the weather back one step at a time.

A fair plan asks your dog for one hard thing at a time. A big, thick-coated dog may stroll into sleet without a second look, and holding your Shih Tzu to that standard sets them up to lose. The tool below sorts your recent outings into a next step. Its four-out-of-five threshold is our practical rule of thumb, so adjust it to the dog in front of you.

Which step should you practice today?

Think about your last five bad-weather outings. Tick each one where your Shih Tzu peed or pooped where you wanted.

Successful outings

Before you start, write down what “success” means in your home. Peeing counts. So does pooping. Stepping outside without going is progress worth a kind word, and it should stay a separate line in your notes so you don't mistake it for an empty bladder.

McCann Dog Training shows how to put a word to the act of going, so you have something familiar to offer when the weather is unfamiliar. Watch the timing of the word and the reward.

Why has my Shih Tzu suddenly stopped going outside?

Usually the place no longer looks or feels like the toilet your dog learned. Rain, cold ground, wind or snow change the surface, the smell and the comfort of the same patch of yard.

In a PetMD article on snow and rain refusals, veterinarian Lori Pasternak points out that terrain can look, smell and feel different, and that a dog taught to go on grass may be confused when no grass is visible. Trainer Brandi Barker adds in the same piece that some dogs with tiny paws or thin coats find cold stressful enough to freeze up and not go at all. A long Shih Tzu coat does not tell you how your individual dog feels about wet feet, so watch the dog and not the breed label.

Look for the point where the trip breaks

Refusal is not a single behavior. One dog plants at the threshold. Another steps out, stands on the one dry paving slab and stares back at you. A third walks willingly, sniffs for ages and brings it all home. Each of those needs a different fix, which is why the planner above asks where things stall. If your dog balks at outings in dry weather too, read the guide to a Shih Tzu who hates walks, because that problem starts somewhere other than the forecast.

Check what else changed that week

Weather is the obvious suspect and it may not be the only one. Think about a new coat or harness, a different door, a slippery step, a security light that clicks on, new neighbors, or a rushed timetable. Note it all. A change in toileting can also have a health cause, and the RSPCA's toilet training advice recommends having a vet rule that out when indoor toileting is a concern or training is not working. Make that call early if the change came with no clear trigger, if it happens in fine weather as well, or if your dog seems unlike themselves.

How do you retrain a Shih Tzu that won't potty outside?

Go back to basic house training for a while: frequent chances, close supervision, one spot, one cue and an immediate reward. Then add the difficult weather in small pieces.

Step 1: Set up a spot your dog can say yes to

Pick the place closest to the door that your dog already knows, and give it protection from wind and falling rain. Whole Dog Journal's cold weather house training column by trainer Pat Miller suggests a sheltered spot near the house, tall enough for you to stand under as well. A porch overhang, a patio umbrella weighted so it cannot move, or a wind screen along one side can do the job. Keep a bit of the surface your dog knows underneath, whether that is grass, mulch or gravel.

Step 2: Go out too

Opening the door and hoping rarely tells you anything. Miller's advice is to go with your dog, because you can keep them on task, use the cue and know whether they actually went. Put your boots, umbrella, treats and leash by the door the night before. An owner who is ready can stand still and relaxed; an owner hunting for a glove tends to tug.

Step 3: Give one cue, then wait quietly

Say your chosen word once as your dog starts to circle or sniff. Teach it first on pleasant days, when going is easy, so the word already means something when conditions are poor. Then stand mostly still. Choose a waiting window before you leave the house; five minutes is a reasonable starting point that you can shorten or stretch. If the window closes with nothing, go in without sighing or commenting.

Step 4: Reward outdoors, at once

Pay the moment your dog finishes, while you are both still outside. The PetMD piece recommends a treat your dog does not get all the time, given immediately, so the connection is clear. Carry it in a closed pocket pouch where you can reach it with cold fingers. A reward handed over in the kitchen mostly rewards coming back into the kitchen.

Step 5: Make the indoor wait short and supervised

After an empty trip, keep your dog in the room with you, on a leash attached to you, or in a small comfortable area. Try again after a short interval. Freedom of the house returns after they have gone outside. This protects your floor and, more importantly, removes the chance to practice the indoor habit you are trying to replace.

What does a bad-weather potty day look like?

Build the day around the moments when your dog is most likely to need to go. The RSPCA lists these for puppies: on waking, before bed, after every meal, after play or exercise, after excitement, before you leave and when you get back, then every 45 minutes to an hour depending on age. Adults generally keep a looser rhythm, but the same moments are good places to look.

Use movement when standing still fails

The Peninsula Humane Society & SPCA published a rainy day potty column written in the voice of a Boston terrier. The owner in it found that a short leash walk worked when the backyard did not: coat on, big umbrella, treats in a pocket, towel by the door, a familiar spot, the “go potty” cue, no rushing, and room to keep sniffing if the first place is not right. Try this when your dog stalls in the yard but perks up at the sight of the leash. Keep it brief and turn for home once the job is done on a miserable day. If your dog loves walks, wait a few more minutes after they go, so that finishing does not instantly end the fun.

Should you use a raincoat, umbrella or indoor potty?

Use whichever removes the specific thing your dog objects to. Introduce each aid on a dry day, because new gear plus bad weather is two lessons at once.

Is an indoor option giving up?

It is a legitimate choice for a high-rise, a mobility limit or a long stretch of harsh winter. Miller's column discusses litter boxes, pads and grass-style trays, and notes that you can teach the box outdoors on good days before moving it inside. Decide which arrangement you want. A pad that appears at random on stormy nights teaches your dog that the rules shift, and that makes next week harder.

A seven-outing checklist for the first wet week

  1. Outing 1: Set the covered spot. Walk there, reward arrival, and accept whatever happens.
  2. Outing 2: Add your cue once. Time your waiting window.
  3. Outing 3: Reward on the spot if your dog goes. If not, come in, supervise and note the time.
  4. Outing 4: Fix the one stall point you noticed: doorway, ground or distraction.
  5. Outing 5: Keep everything the same so you can see whether the fix worked.
  6. Outing 6: Count your successes and use the planner to choose a step.
  7. Outing 7: If you moved up, change only one thing, such as the distance from the door or the amount of cover.

Seven outings can pass in a couple of days. That is fine, since the list counts attempts and gives you a clean record, and it makes no promise about when the habit will be solid. A line such as “7:10, drizzle, stalled on step, went on second try” is enough. Share the note with everyone who takes the dog out so one person's dry-day shortcut doesn't undo another's practice.

After you come back in

Blot paws, belly and the hair around the rear with a towel, using presses instead of vigorous rubbing so the long coat doesn't twist into knots. Make drying part of the reward routine, with a treat and a pause. A dog who dislikes the towel may start dreading the whole trip; the cooperative handling plan shows how to let your dog opt in a little at a time.

Which mistakes keep the problem going?

Punishing the indoor puddle

The RSPCA advises never punishing a dog for an accident, whether you find it later or catch it happening, because the dog can become scared and confused. If you see your dog start, call their name to interrupt kindly, take them calmly to the right place and praise them if they finish there.

Leaving a scent reminder

Clean accidents fully. The RSPCA suggests a warm solution of one part biological washing powder to nine parts water, followed by a rinse, to remove the smell. Test a hidden corner of fabric or carpet first, and keep your dog away until it is dry.

Turning the trip into a standoff

Repeating the cue, leaning over your dog or tugging toward the grass adds pressure. In the PetMD article, Barker advises a deep breath and no impatient gestures. A short, calm failure that you repeat later is better than twenty tense minutes. PetMD also quotes Pasternak warning against long stays outdoors on very cold days.

Changing everything at once

New coat, new boots, new spot and new treat on the same stormy night leave you with no idea what helped. Your dog faces four surprises. Change one, give it several outings, then decide.

Relaxing the routine after one good day

One success in drizzle is welcome and it is only one. Keep rewarding for a while, and return to the covered step when a harder storm arrives. Going back a step is part of a fair plan, with no penalty for either of you.

Frequently asked questions

Why will my Shih Tzu pee outside in dry weather but not in rain?

The trip feels different. Falling rain, a wet surface, cold paws and a hurried owner can each change it. Change one of those at a time, starting with cover over the usual spot, and note which change gets your dog to go.

Should I make my Shih Tzu stay out until they go?

No. Wait for a short, set window, then come in without comment, supervise closely and try again soon. Long standoffs on very cold days are something to avoid.

Is it okay to use a pee pad on bad weather days?

It can be, if you choose it on purpose. Put it in one fixed place, reward its use, and expect to teach the difference between the pad and other soft things on the floor.

Do I need a raincoat or boots for my Shih Tzu?

Only if your dog is comfortable in them. Introduce each item indoors on dry days first. A coat that arrives for the first time during a downpour adds one more strange thing to the trip.

What should I do when I find an accident?

Clean it up and skip the scolding. Then shorten the gap before the next outing and keep your dog where you can see them until they have gone outside.

How long does retraining take?

There is no fixed number of days. Move to the next step when your dog succeeds on most recent outings, and drop back one step after a rough patch.

Give your dog a fair next try

Pick the covered spot today, put the treats by the door and notice exactly where the next outing stalls. A Shih Tzu who won't potty outside in bad weather needs a version of the trip they can manage, followed by a slightly harder one when they are ready.
