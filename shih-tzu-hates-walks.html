Why Your Shih Tzu Hates Walks (and How to Get Them Walking Happily)

Why the walk stopped working for your dog, and a fair, force-free plan to fix gear, routes, pace and training.

If your Shih Tzu hates walks, plants all four paws on the sidewalk, or spins back toward the front door, the dog is not being “stubborn” for sport—it is telling you that something about the walk is not working for them. A small, flat-faced companion dog has a different set of limits than the Labrador next door, and the fair response is to listen before you pull.

Quick answer: Most walk refusal comes down to five things: discomfort (nails, paws, a sore body), gear that presses on the neck or rubs, heat and weather, fear of the route, and walks that are simply too long or too fast. Rule out a sudden physical change with your vet first, switch from a collar to a well-fitted harness, walk in the cooler part of the day, shorten sessions to a few minutes, let the dog sniff, and reward every forward step instead of dragging.

Below we go through each cause, the gear that tends to help, how to pick routes and pace, and a step-by-step plan that rebuilds a dog’s willingness to walk without force.

Why does a Shih Tzu refuse to walk?

A Shih Tzu refuses to walk because the walk costs them more than it gives: something hurts, something is scary, they are too hot or too tired, or the walk has no reward in it for them. “Stubborn” is a label for the owner’s frustration, not an explanation of the dog’s behavior.

Trainer and writer John Strassburger’s Whole Dog Journal guide to leash refusal lists the usual suspects in a sensible order: physical discomfort such as nails that are too long or sore skin between the toes, a harness that fits badly, unpleasant surfaces like hot pavement or prickly grass, walks that rush the dog and deny sniffing time, and fear of sights and sounds along the way. It is worth working through them in that order, because the cheapest fixes come first.

Discomfort you cannot see

Shih Tzus have heavily furred feet, so long nails and matted hair between the pads hide easily. A dog that walks normally on carpet but shortens its stride on concrete is often telling you about its feet. If the dislike of walking appeared suddenly, treat that as a reason to see your vet before you change anything else; a training plan cannot fix pain, and pushing through it would be unfair to the dog.

A flat face changes the math

The Shih Tzu is a brachycephalic breed. Wikipedia’s breed overview notes that dogs affected by brachycephalic airway obstruction syndrome struggle with exercise and are prone to heat stroke because they cool themselves poorly. Our article on Shih Tzu breathing problems and severity signs explains what noisy breathing on a walk can mean. A dog that sits down halfway through a warm walk may simply be doing the sensible thing.

Fear and overwhelm

A small dog sees the world from the height of car bumpers and garden gates. A single bad experience—a truck’s air brakes, a lunging dog behind a fence—can turn a whole street into a place to avoid. Refusal that always happens at the same spot is usually about that spot.

The clearest short demonstration of the training side is from Happy Hounds Dog Training. Is it ever right to pull or drag a Shih Tzu that won’t walk?

No. Dragging teaches the dog that the leash is something to fear, puts pressure on a small neck, and turns a confidence problem into a trust problem. The right move is to stop, make it easy to move forward, and reward the first step.

This is the ethical core of walk training. A walk is supposed to be good for the dog, so a walk that has to be forced has already failed its purpose. Whole Dog Journal is blunt about it: never drag the dog, do not force them toward places that make them nervous, and use treats and praise instead. Respecting a “no” does not mean giving up on walks; it means finding out what the no is about and changing the deal until the answer becomes yes.

What to do in the moment

  • Stop and let the leash go slack. Tension invites the dog to brace against it.
  • Look around for the trigger: a noise, a dog, a surface, the heat.
  • Turn sideways or take one step in a different direction, then cheerfully invite the dog along.
  • Reward the moment the dog moves forward, not while they are sitting—otherwise you pay for the stop.
  • If the dog is clearly frightened or overheated, pick them up and end the walk calmly. That is a kindness, not a defeat.

What is the best gear for a Shih Tzu that hates walks?

A well-fitted, non-tightening harness with a standard fixed-length leash is the best starting point for most Shih Tzus. Flat collars put pressure on the neck of a flat-faced dog, and retractable leashes teach the dog that pulling earns more line.

The evidence on this is clearer than many owners expect. A 2025 review by Cavalli and Protopopova in the journal Animals, summarized by the research portal Faunalytics, looked at 21 articles and two abstracts on walking equipment. It concluded that flat collars should not be used on brachycephalic dogs, reported that brachycephalic dogs had raised pressure inside the eye when walking in collars but not in harnesses, and recommended reward-based training plus a non-tightening front-clip harness for dogs that pull. That eye-pressure point matters for a breed with prominent eyes—see our overview of how common Shih Tzu eye problems are.

How a harness should fit

A harness should let you slide two fingers under every strap, sit clear of the throat, and not touch the armpits when the dog walks. Long coats get trapped in buckles and Velcro, so brush the chest and armpit hair before fitting. If your dog runs when the harness appears, the gear itself has become the problem; teach them to put their head through it for a treat before you ever clip a leash on. The same slow, reward-based handling approach we use for grooming biting works for harness shyness.

Face hair and visibility

A Shih Tzu with hair falling into the eyes is walking half-blind, and a dog that cannot see well is more likely to freeze at new things. A neat topknot or a tidy face trim helps—our guide on how often to groom a Shih Tzu face covers the routine.

How far and how fast should a Shih Tzu walk?

As far and as fast as your own dog can do comfortably while still sniffing, breathing quietly and wanting to continue. There is no single correct distance for the breed, and Whole Dog Journal points out that not every dog needs long walks.

Many walk problems are pace problems. A person walking briskly covers ground the dog has to trot to keep up with, and a short-nosed dog trotting on a warm day is working hard. Let the dog set the speed and treat sniffing as part of the exercise rather than an interruption. Sniffing is how a dog “reads the news,” and it is a large part of why walks are worth having from the dog’s point of view.

Signs the walk is too much

  • Loud or rasping breathing, or panting that does not settle when you pause
  • Lagging behind, lying down in shade, or turning firmly for home
  • A tongue that looks wider than usual, or the dog seeking cool ground
  • Stiffness or reluctance the next morning

If you see these, shorten the next walk rather than pushing through. Two or three short outings a day are often a better fit for this breed than one long one.

When is it too hot to walk a Shih Tzu?

For a flat-faced dog, warm and humid weather is a real risk, not just a comfort issue. Walk early in the morning or late in the evening, keep warm-day walks short and shaded, and skip the walk entirely on hot days.

The Royal Veterinary College’s brachycephaly research page states that brachycephalic breeds are at least twice as likely to suffer from heat-related illness, with some breeds, such as the English Bulldog, facing a 14-fold increase in risk. The Shih Tzu has the same kind of shortened airway, which is why heat should shape your walking timetable for half the year.

Weather checklist before you open the door

  • Touch the pavement with the back of your hand. If it is uncomfortable for you, it is uncomfortable for paws.
  • Pick grass or shaded paths over open asphalt on sunny days.
  • Bring water and plan a route with places to rest.
  • In cold, wet weather, dry the feet and belly afterward; a soaked long coat chills a small dog fast, and cold paws are another reason dogs refuse.

Which routes make walking easier?

Quiet, predictable routes with soft ground, shade and plenty to sniff. Start close to home, avoid known trigger spots while confidence is low, and only add busier places once your Shih Tzu walks happily on the easy ones.

Think of routes as levels in a game. Level one might be your hallway or yard. Level two is the stretch of sidewalk outside your door at a quiet time. Level three is a short loop around the block. A noisy main road, a dog park entrance or a school at pick-up time comes much later. If your dog refuses at the door, consider carrying them a short distance to a calmer starting point and walking back toward home—many dogs walk eagerly in that direction.

Turning a trigger spot into a neutral one

If refusal always happens at the same place, approach it from a distance where your dog still takes treats, reward heavily as you get closer and as you pass, and stop the session while the dog is still relaxed. Whole Dog Journal notes that refusing even high-value treats is a sign of high stress; that is your signal to back up, not to push on.

Step-by-step: rebuilding a Shih Tzu who hates walks

This plan follows the reward-based sequence described by Whole Dog Journal and the Happy Hounds video. Move to the next step only when your dog is relaxed and keen at the current one.

  1. Check the body. Trim nails, clear matted hair from between the pads, and book a vet visit if the refusal is new.
  2. Fix the gear. Swap the collar for a well-fitted harness and a light fixed-length leash. Put the retractable away.
  3. Make the leash a good sign. Say “Leash!”, clip it on, and give a treat immediately. Repeat a few times a day without going anywhere.
  4. Teach a “let’s walk” cue indoors. Use an upbeat word such as “Walk,” take one step, and reward when the dog follows. Keep these practice sessions to about 3–5 minutes, as Whole Dog Journal suggests.
  5. Fade the lure. Start with a treat in your hand to show the way, then say the cue before you show the treat, then reward from a pouch only after the dog moves—the same three phases shown in the video.
  6. Take it outside, briefly. Walk a few houses at a quiet time, reward often, and go home while your dog still wants more.
  7. Grow distance slowly. Add a little length or one new street at a time. If enthusiasm drops, go back a step for a few days.
  8. Keep walks worth it. Sniff breaks, new smells, a friendly neighbor, a treat scatter on the grass: the dog should finish each walk feeling it was a good deal.

If your Shih Tzu is still a puppy, mix these steps into the short outdoor trips you already make for toileting; our Shih Tzu puppy potty training timeline shows how those trips change with age.

Common mistakes that make walking worse

  • Paying for the stop. Walking back to a sitting dog and handing over a treat rewards sitting. Reward the first forward step instead.
  • Using a collar on a flat-faced dog. The 2025 review advised against flat collars for brachycephalic breeds.
  • Walking at human speed. A brisk pace and no sniffing turns a walk into a chore.
  • Ignoring the weather. Midday summer walks are the riskiest thing a Shih Tzu owner can do on a regular basis.
  • Doing too much too soon. One great five-minute walk beats a miserable half hour.
  • Labeling the dog stubborn. The label stops you from looking for the real reason.
  • Giving up on walks entirely. Carrying or strolling everywhere means the dog never gets to practice; use these as support, not replacement.

Frequently asked questions

Why does my Shih Tzu sit down and refuse to walk?

Common reasons are discomfort (long nails, sore paws, an aching body), a collar or harness that presses or rubs, heat, fear of something on the route, and walks that are too long or too fast. If the behavior started suddenly, see your vet first.

Should I use a collar or a harness on a Shih Tzu?

Use a harness for walking. A 2025 review of walking equipment concluded that flat collars should not be used on brachycephalic dogs and recommended a non-tightening front-clip harness plus reward-based training for dogs that pull.

How long should I walk a Shih Tzu that hates walks?

Start with very short sessions of about three to five minutes of practice, end while the dog is still keen, and add distance gradually. Several short outings often suit the breed better than one long walk.

Is it okay to carry my Shih Tzu when they refuse to walk?

Yes, when the dog is scared, tired or too warm. Carrying past a trigger or to a calmer starting point is fair. Just avoid making carrying the default, or the dog never practices walking.

Why does my Shih Tzu only walk toward home?

Home is safe and familiar, and the outbound part of a walk is where the unknowns are. Try starting a short distance from home and walking back, then gradually extend the outward leg with lots of rewards.

Is it safe to walk a Shih Tzu in hot weather?

Be cautious. Flat-faced breeds are at least twice as likely to suffer heat-related illness according to the Royal Veterinary College. Walk early or late, stay in shade, keep it short, and skip hot days.

The fair way to a happy walker

A Shih Tzu that hates walks is giving you honest feedback. Check the body, fix the gear, respect the heat, slow down, choose easier routes and reward every step in the right direction. Doing it the patient way takes a little longer than dragging, but it builds a dog who chooses to walk with you—which is the whole point.
