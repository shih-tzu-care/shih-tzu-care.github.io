Shih Tzu Crate at Night vs. Sleeping in Your Bed

If you are weighing a Shih Tzu crate at night versus sleeping in your bed, picture what happens after the first wake-up. A puppy who naps beside you in daylight still has to learn where to settle after a nighttime toilet trip, and you need a setup you can use when you are tired.

Quick answer

There is no standard age. VCA Animal Hospitals notes that crate training can start at any age, and Dogs Trust says every dog is different. Start with a familiar floor-level spot in the same room, keep the nighttime toilet routine steady, and change the overnight arrangement once your dog settles there repeatedly.

At what age do owners move a Shih Tzu from a crate to their bed?

There is no standard age. VCA Animal Hospitals notes that crate training can start at any age and that most dogs adjust to crate confinement easily. Dogs Trust adds that every dog is different, so the right moment comes from what your dog shows you, not from a calendar.

Why a birthday is a weak guide

A move to the bed changes three things at once: the surface your Shih Tzu rests on, how close you are, and whether the dog can reach you during the night. A dog who settles in a crate at eight weeks and a dog who settles at eight months are both telling you the same thing: this setup works for me right now. Dogs Trust's crate guidance builds door practice gradually and watches for relaxed body language at every stage, which is the same signal you need before opening up the bed.

Instead of an age, record the conditions around the change: the old sleeping spot, the new one, how your dog settled, the nighttime toilet trips, and what happened after the first wake-up. That log turns “she was fine” into a pattern you can compare across a week.

What a calm nighttime toilet routine looks like

The same VCA guide suggests that a puppy who wakes and fidgets may need to eliminate. It recommends a quiet, on-leash toilet break without play, then a return to the sleeping spot and a small treat, and it notes that young puppies often need one nighttime break until they can hold their bladder and bowels through the night. Keep those cues the same in every setup, so changing where your dog sleeps does not also change how nighttime toilet trips work.

When you compare notes with other owners, write down the dog's age at the switch, the old sleeping spot, the new one, and what happened after the move. A post saying “she sleeps with me now” leaves the timing unanswered. A post about a successful first evening leaves the rest of the week unknown. Use the examples to think through two possible outcomes: your dog settles quickly, or your dog treats the new access as an invitation to interact.

What changes when you open the crate door or share the bed?

You can change access, sleeping location, and proximity to people independently. An open crate next to your bed changes the door rule while keeping the familiar resting surface. A dog bed beside you gives you proximity and a separate sleep surface. An invitation onto your bed also changes how easily the dog can reach you during the night.

Choose the change you are trying to make

If your goal is keeping your Shih Tzu close, try moving their floor bed beside yours first. If your goal is giving them a choice of resting spots, start with the crate door open in the prepared bedroom. If your goal is bed sharing, keep a floor option and agree with everyone who uses the bed about invitations. Treat “where should we sleep?” and “how do we respond at night?” as two decisions. You can answer the second before trying the first.

Consider the bedroom from floor level. A basket by the bed, an open hallway, and the gap under a dresser can all turn into destinations when the crate door opens. Decide which space you are offering and clear it before the trial. If your bed arrangement gives your small dog no practical way to get down, choose the floor bed for overnight practice. The ability to leave is part of the setup you are evaluating.

How do you know your Shih Tzu is ready to try a different sleeping spot?

Look for repeatable settling while you are awake, a toilet routine that carries over, and a place your dog can use without needing you to continually reposition them. Add the overnight trial after you have tested those pieces during quieter evenings.

Observe a routine you can repeat

Pick a familiar quiet period after the last planned outing. Put the floor bed in its proposed nighttime position. Watch whether your dog approaches it, lies down, and returns to rest after an ordinary movement such as you turning off a lamp. Note what happened in plain language. “Lay down, got up when I stood, then returned on her own” gives you more to work with than a pass or fail label.

Use the same last outing and response to waking that already work for your puppy. If you are still finding that schedule, the Shih Tzu potty training timeline can help you plan the house training side separately. For a puppy who wakes and needs an outing, the next step might simply be rehearsing a quiet return to the bedside mat after that outing.

If your puppy is new to the household, Dogs Trust recommends beginning nights in the same room with a bed, pen, or open crate. Their crate guidance builds up door movement and closed-door time gradually, while the dog is relaxed. Practice these skills in daylight and use an arrangement the dog can already handle when you need to sleep.

Give the dog a choice about closeness

A Shih Tzu who leaves the duvet for the floor has shown you which place they wanted at that moment. Keep the floor spot available even if you enjoy having them beside you. Our guide to bonding with a Shih Tzu who prefers space has more ways to build a relaxed routine around their choices.

A step-by-step transition you can reverse

  1. Keep a baseline. Write down the current sleep spot, how the dog settles, the usual wake-ups, and what you do after each one. If another person takes the late toilet trip, ask them what they see. Agree on the part of the routine you will keep unchanged during the trial.
  2. Introduce the alternate spot before bedtime. Put the mat or bed where you want it, allow your dog to investigate, and reward relaxed use. Battersea’s settle exercise starts by rewarding interest in the mat, then builds time and distance at the dog's pace. Begin while you are sitting nearby and end while the exercise is still easy.
  3. Try a quiet stretch in the bedroom. Use the usual bedtime cues while you are still awake and able to observe. If your dog checks the doorway and returns to the mat, record that. If they repeat a route around the room or keep asking to get up, shorten the next attempt and practice the floor spot again.
  4. Rehearse the return from a break. After an ordinary toilet outing, walk back to the proposed sleep area and let your dog settle again. Keep the lights and conversation low. This is a separate practice opportunity because a dog who lies down at first may want to check the room again after coming back in.
  5. Make one reversible overnight change. A floor bed or familiar open crate in the same room is a useful first change. For bed sharing, begin with an invitation during quiet time while you are awake. Keep the separate floor spot usable, and choose your overnight setup before you switch the lights off.
  6. Compare the next morning. Ask where the dog settled initially, what happened after each wake-up, and how much intervention people needed. If the change added repeated interruptions, return to the last well-tolerated setup. Practice the missing part during the day and let subsequent observations decide when to try again.

What if the new sleep spot creates more work?

Return to the last arrangement where your dog could relax and the household could sleep. Use the pattern of interruptions to choose a smaller practice task. If the dog repeatedly tries to leave confinement or becomes more upset, end that trial and seek help from a reward-based trainer if the pattern continues.

Work on the wake-up you actually saw

Suppose your dog settles on your bed at lights-out but repeatedly comes to your pillow later. Keep the nighttime toilet response available. Then rehearse returning to the floor spot before attempting another shared night. If they already sleep calmly in a bedside bed, you may decide that this is the long-term arrangement and keep cuddles for a time when everyone is awake.

In a home with two dogs, look at both resting patterns. One dog getting up may get the other moving too. Start with distinct places for each dog and observe whether they can disengage. For planning how the spaces fit together, see our guide to setting up separate resting areas for two dogs.

Common mistakes that make bedtime harder to learn

  • Using a birthday as a deadline. Keep age in the log for context and use recent observations to choose the next exercise.
  • Changing the door, room, and sleep surface together. Change one part first so you can tell which change the dog is learning.
  • Starting only when everyone is exhausted. Agree on the fallback and practice the invitation or mat cue while people can respond consistently.
  • Making the crate a consequence. Maintain it as an optional familiar resting place. Close the door only during gradual practice your dog is comfortable with.
  • Assuming silence answers every question. Record whether the dog actually lies down, how they return after outings, and whether people can rest too.

Frequently asked questions

Is there a recommended age to start sharing the bed?

No single age fits every dog. VCA notes crate training can start at any age, and Dogs Trust says every dog is different. Choose when to practice based on how your dog settles, the toilet routine, and whether everyone can rest in the proposed setup.

Can a Shih Tzu sleep in their own bed permanently?

Yes. A floor bed in your room can be the long-term arrangement. Practice a calm return to that spot after a toilet outing and leave it available even on evenings when your dog is invited onto your bed.

Should I get rid of the crate after switching?

If your dog already chooses to rest there, keep the crate available with its door secured open. You can still practice brief, relaxed use separately from the overnight routine.

Can I switch back after a difficult night?

Return to whichever familiar arrangement allowed your dog to relax. Look at what changed and practice that piece while you are awake. If confinement itself repeatedly upsets the dog, end the trial and ask a reward-based trainer for help.

What if my Shih Tzu moves around after lights-out?

Observe when they get up and what happens next. Keep the usual toilet response, then practice the return to a floor mat during an easier evening. Try another overnight change once that return is familiar.

Can daytime naps tell me when my dog is ready?

A voluntary nap is a useful starting observation. Also practice settling after your own movements and after a toilet trip, since those are parts of the overnight routine you will need to repeat.

Choose a practice step for tonight

Put the preferred resting spot beside the familiar setup, run your usual evening routine, and watch what your Shih Tzu does after the first interruption. A calm return to rest gives you a reason to repeat that setup. If it takes repeated prompting, make the next session easier and keep the overnight arrangement that already works.
